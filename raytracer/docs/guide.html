SOVS optics bench: user guide

An interactive paraxial ray-tracer for the OPTOM 263 Essential Optics course (School of Optometry and Vision Science, University of Auckland). You build or load an optical system (a lens, a telescope, a schematic eye, an instrument), place objects in front of it, and see where the images form, drag things about, and read the numbers.

It uses paraxial (small-angle) optics and the sign conventions used in optometry. It is a teaching tool: the rays are exact for the paraxial model and say nothing about aberrations.

1. What it does

  • Draws a lens system (thin lenses, thick lenses, surfaces, mirrors, prisms, an "img" screen such as a retina) from a table of its elements, with its cardinal points (focal F and F', principal P and P', nodal N and N'), its pupils (E and E') and its vertices (V and V').
  • Places objects in front of it: a point at a finite distance, or a beam from infinity at an angle. For each it traces the rays through every element and shows the image: real or virtual, where it is, how big, which way up.
  • Lets you drag the object, the image or the beam angle on the diagram and watches the table follow, or type numbers into the table and watches the diagram follow.
  • Optionally models dispersion: give each medium a material and each object a wavelength, and watch different colours focus at different places (white light, chromatic aberration).

4. Objects and images

Every object has a row in Objects and Images and a drawing on the diagram. There are two kinds:

  • A point at a finite distance (the infinity box ∞ is not ticked). Rays leave the point and spread through the system. You give its position (l, or lv) and height (h). The image is a point (real or virtual).
  • A beam from infinity (∞ ticked). Parallel rays arriving at an angle θ. Only the angle and the beam width matter; the image forms on the back focal plane (F') at a height set by the angle.

To add one use the New menu under the table (point, object, white-light group, or the older source / beam / afocal beam entries), or load a model that already has them. To remove one, tick its row and press Delete Selected; Clear removes them all and Reset puts back the ones the model came with.

A finite object has a red dot (the object) and a cyan dot (its image). A beam has a cyan dot (its image on F') and a small handle at the lens (the angle picker) that you turn to change the angle.

4.1 What the columns mean

Column Meaning
∞ Tick for a beam from infinity; untick for a point at a finite distance.
Type object (rays from a point, or a beam) or point (just the three principal rays through the cardinal points). Click to change.
λ (nm) The wavelength of this object (only with Advanced materials on).
Group Link objects together (see white light).
l, l' Object and image distances, measured from the principal planes P and P'.
lv, lv' Object and image distances, measured from the vertices V and V'.
h, h' Object and image heights.
θ, θ' The beam angle (object side) and the image-space angle.
beam width The width of the beam entering the system.
Pin Fix the beam width so it just fills the aperture stop.
Vig Use your beam width, and let whichever elements vignette it clip the beam.
Hide Switch the beam off the diagram (the row stays).

Distances are in the unit chosen in the box beside the tabs (mm by default); powers are always dioptres. An empty cell or -- means "not defined here", for example the image of an object at the front focal point is at infinity.

4.2 Real, virtual and the colours

  • Solid lines are real light. Dashed lines are construction: where the rays would meet (a virtual image or a virtual object) or where a beam goes on to an object at infinity.
  • A short dashed boundary line marks the place where real light turns into construction.
  • A beam's shading fades out towards infinity.
  • With Advanced materials on, each object's beam takes the colour of its wavelength (grey for a Custom object).

Figure 3. A plus lens with the object inside the focal point F. The rays leave diverging, so the image (cyan) is virtual: it is where they seem to come from, reached along dashed lines. The red dot is the object.

5. Moving things on the diagram

Use the Pointer tool and drag:

  • The red dot (object). Moves a finite object; the image and the table follow.
  • The cyan dot (image). Moves the image; the object is recalculated to match. For a beam from infinity the cyan dot moves only up and down the focal plane, which changes the angle.
  • The angle picker (beams). Turn it to change the angle θ.
  • A beam's edge (the incoming edges of a beam). Drag it towards or away from the centre to make the beam narrower or wider. (Not while Pin fixes the width.)

5.1 Snapping to the cardinal points

A dragged point is held on V, V', F, F', P, P', N or N' when it is within a few pixels of one, or passes one. Pull past it to carry on. Hold Alt to turn the snapping off. A drag that is purely up and down never snaps along the axis.

The back focal plane F' is special: an image on F' belongs to an object at infinity. Drag an image there and the object is held a very large distance away, l shows as infinite (--), and the beam is drawn collimated, with dashed construction lines on to the object at infinity. For the relaxed eye F' lies just in front of the retina (V'), and the snap prefers F'.

Figure 4. An image dragged on to F' (the retina of the relaxed eye). The object is at infinity: the rays arrive parallel, and the dashed lines run on, in the direction they came from, to the object at infinity.

5.2 Grid

Positions you drag to are rounded to a small grid (0.025 mm), so the numbers stay tidy.

6. Reading the diagram

  • V, V' the first and last vertices of the system. F, F' the focal points. P, P' the principal points. N, N' the nodal points. E, E' the entrance and exit pupils. The dashed vertical lines through the cardinal points are the planes. Turn any of these off in the show menu.

Figure 5. A thick plus lens with its labelled points: the focal points F and F', the principal / nodal points P/N and P'/N', the vertices V and V', and the entrance and exit pupils E/E'. The dashed vertical lines are the planes through the focal points.

  • A screen ("img" element, for example a retina) stops the light: nothing is drawn beyond it except the dashed construction to an image behind it.
  • Intermediate images inside a system (for example in a telescope) are marked I1, I2 ...

7. The tables

7.1 Lens Prescription

One row per element: type (surface, thin lens, thick lens, prism, stop, mirror, screen), the refractive index after it, the radius, the thickness to the next element, the power, the aperture diameter and a Stop Flag. Edit a cell and the diagram redraws. The buttons under the table add a refractive index (a medium), thin lens, thick lens, spherical surface or thin prism; Delete Selected, Clear and Reset remove elements or put back the model's own. The wavelength button beside the unit box shows which wavelength the Ref. Index column is for (with Advanced materials on).

Figure 6. The Lens Prescription tab for the Le Grand relaxed eye: one row per element.

7.2 Summary

The paraxial properties of the whole system, in a set of small tables:

Table Contents
Summary The refractive index in front of (n) and behind (n') the system, and its length VV'.
Powers (D) The equivalent power F, and the front and back vertex powers Fv and Fv'.
Distances (mm) The focal lengths (PF, P'F'), the vertex focal lengths (VF, V'F'), and the positions of the principal points (VP, V'P') and nodal points (VN, V'N'), front and back.
Pupils (mm) The entrance and exit pupils: position (VE, VE') and magnification.
System matrix The ABCD ray-transfer matrix of the whole system (A, B, C, D).

The small copy button beside a value puts it on the clipboard. With Advanced materials on, the Summary is for the wavelength of the object in focus (the wavelength button beside the unit box says which); the pupils and cardinal points drawn on the diagram are for the same wavelength.

Figure 7. The Summary tab for a thick plus lens (power 10 D).

A .lens file can hide prescription columns; see ADVANCED-MATERIALS.md.

8. Settings, wavelengths and white light

Everything that changes how the bench behaves, rather than what is on the bench, is in Settings: the cog in the top bar. The options take effect at once and are remembered in your browser; Done closes the dialog.

8.1 The Settings dialog

Figure 8. The Settings dialog.

Option What it does Default
Advanced materials Turns on materials and wavelengths (see 8.2). Off, the program behaves as a plain paraxial ray-tracer with the indices you type. Off
Light diagram / Dark diagram The colour of the diagram. The sun / moon switch in the top bar does the same. Light
Additive beams Overlapping beams add up like light: red, green and blue together come out white. Needs the dark diagram (it is greyed out on the light one). Off
Beam edge lines Draws a line along the edge of every shaded beam (incoming, between the elements and outgoing). Off, a beam is just its shading. Works on either diagram. Off
Wavelengths... Opens the list of wavelengths the program offers (see 8.3).

Other controls you may be looking for are not in the dialog: the distance unit box beside the tabs (µm, mm, cm, m; powers stay in dioptres), the Show menu and tool rail on the diagram, and the Model button.

8.2 Advanced materials

Off, every refractive index is the fixed number typed into the prescription table, at every wavelength: the textbook model. Switched on:

  • The Material column appears in the Lens Prescription table. Name a material for a medium (and for a thin lens or prism) instead of typing an index. The materials are Air, Water, Ocular media, Fused silica, PMMA, CR-39, BK7, Crown 1.523, Trivex, Polycarbonate, High index 1.60 / 1.67 / 1.74, F2 flint and SF11 dense flint. Custom keeps whatever fixed index you type.
  • Each object gets a wavelength (the lambda column of Objects and Images) and is traced through the index its materials have at that wavelength, so blue and red light focus in different places.
  • A link column appears, for linking objects into a group (see 8.4).
  • A wavelength button appears beside the unit box and beside the Summary: it shows which wavelength the Ref. Index column and the Summary are for, and follows the object in focus.
  • Each object's beam is drawn in the colour of its wavelength (grey for Custom).

The index at a wavelength comes from a two-term Cauchy fit through the material's refractive index at the d line and its Abbe number; radii and powers you type are for the d line (587.6 nm). The full description, and the extra keys a .lens file may use, are in ADVANCED-MATERIALS.md.

Figure 9. With Advanced materials on, each wavelength is traced through the index it has: white light through an SF11 prism and a lens separates into its colours.

8.3 The Wavelengths dialog

Figure 10. The Wavelengths dialog (Settings > Wavelengths...).

It lists the wavelengths the program offers when you give an object a wavelength:

Column Meaning
Colour A name for the colour (blue, cyan, green, ...).
Line The name of the spectral line (g, F, d, C, ...).
Wavelength (nm) The wavelength itself. The colour swatch beside it is worked out from this.
Group Tick it to include the wavelength in the white-light group.

Add wavelength adds a row, the x deletes one, Reset to defaults puts the standard list back, Cancel discards your changes and Done keeps them. F (486.1), d (587.6) and C (656.3) are the lines that the Abbe number is defined from.

8.4 White-light groups

A group is a set of linked objects, one for each wavelength of the white-light group (by default blue 435.8 nm, green 514.5 nm and red 656.3 nm):

  • Choose New > white-light group on the Objects and Images tab. A copy of the object is made at each wavelength in the group. (Or tick the link boxes of two objects to link them.)
  • The members are linked: drag one, or change its position, height or angle, and the others follow. You see the dispersion of one scene.
  • The group is made from the ticked Group wavelengths in the Wavelengths dialog, so you can choose another set.

9. Saving and reporting

  • Save (tool rail): the diagram as a PNG, SVG or PDF.
  • Report (top bar): downloads a report of the model and its objects and images, filled in from a template for that lab or assignment. Use it for the lab write-up.
  • The workbench manager (manager.html) can also open and save .lens files on your computer.

10. Choosing and authoring models

  • The Model button opens a searchable list of every model, grouped by topic (components, eyes, instruments, assignments by year). Click to preview, double-click or press Enter to load it.
  • A model is a plain-text .lens file (JSON5) listing the elements of the system, the objects, and optional settings. Examples are in raytracer/lenses/. To try your own, open manager.html and use File > Open; the tables are editable there and the diagram updates, and you can save the result.
  • Which models appear in which lab is set in raytracer/config/config.json (a "profile" per page).

11. Tips

  • Start with the cardinal points. Drag the object to F and watch the image run to infinity; drag it to 2F and the image comes to 2F'.
  • Type exact numbers into l, h or θ when you need an exact value; drag when you want to explore.
  • Dashed means "would". If you cannot see how the light gets there, it probably does not: it is a construction.
  • Untick Pin and tick Vig to see how an aperture or a lens edge clips an oblique beam.
  • If a page looks stale after an update, refresh with the cache cleared (hard refresh).
